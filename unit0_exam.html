Section B · Article Analysis Question

Read the article below, then respond to Parts A–G. 7 marks total.

Social Media Use, Sleep Quality, and Academic Performance in Adolescents: A Correlational Survey Study

Introduction

Adolescence is a developmental period marked by shifting sleep architecture, increasing autonomy over technology use, and mounting academic demands. Over the past decade, smartphone-based social media platforms have become a near-universal feature of adolescent daily life, with many teenagers reporting near-constant connectivity through the evening hours. Two mechanisms have been proposed to link this behaviour to poorer academic functioning. The first is a direct displacement mechanism: time spent scrolling through social media feeds substitutes for time that would otherwise be devoted to homework, studying, or rest, reducing the total hours available for academic preparation. The second is an indirect, sleep-mediated mechanism: late-evening screen exposure delays the onset of sleep through both behavioural displacement (staying awake to keep engaging with content) and physiological arousal (blue-light suppression of melatonin release and psychological stimulation from social content), producing shortened or fragmented sleep that in turn impairs next-day attention, working memory, and emotional regulation — all of which are required for classroom learning and test performance.

Existing evidence linking social media use to academic outcomes has been inconsistent, in part because many prior studies have measured only one link in this proposed chain (for example, screen time and grades, without measuring sleep) rather than testing the full pathway. Sleep researchers have separately established that adolescents who obtain fewer than the recommended eight to ten hours of sleep on school nights show measurable deficits in sustained attention and consolidation of newly learned material. The present study was designed to examine both links simultaneously within a single adolescent sample: the association between social media use and sleep quality, and the association between sleep quality and academic performance, in order to evaluate whether poorer sleep quality helps explain the relationship between heavier social media use and lower academic achievement. Because the design is correlational, the study can establish whether these variables are related and estimate the strength of that relationship, but it cannot by itself establish that social media use causes poorer sleep or that poorer sleep causes lower academic performance.

Participants

Participants were 312 students (162 female, 146 male, 4 identified as non-binary) drawn from Year 10, Year 11, and Year 12 classes at two urban public high schools in the same metropolitan district. Mean age was 16.2 years (SD = 1.1). Students were eligible for inclusion if they owned or had regular access to a personal smartphone or tablet with social media applications installed and had attended their current school for at least one full academic term. Students were excluded if they had a diagnosed sleep disorder requiring clinical treatment (e.g., diagnosed insomnia or sleep apnea under active management) or a documented learning disability that required a modified academic assessment, since either condition could independently affect sleep or performance scores in ways unrelated to social media use. Recruitment took place through school assemblies and homeroom announcements. Because participants were minors, written parental or guardian consent was obtained for every student prior to participation, followed by written student assent immediately before the survey was administered; students were told they could decline to take part even if their parent had consented. The study protocol was approved by the researchers' institutional review board prior to data collection (IRB Protocol #EDU-2025-118).

Method

Three standardised instruments were administered. Social media use was measured using a 10-item Social Media Engagement Scale (SMES; range 10–50, higher scores indicating heavier daily use across platforms, including estimated minutes per day and frequency of checking). Sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; range 0–21, higher scores indicating worse sleep quality), a widely used self-report instrument assessing sleep duration, latency, disturbances, and daytime dysfunction over the previous month. Academic performance was measured using a standardised Academic Performance Composite (APC; range 0–100), which combined each student's most recent cumulative GPA (converted to a 100-point scale) with a standardised in-school assessment of reading and mathematics proficiency administered by the district earlier that term.

All three measures were administered in a single 40-minute survey session completed during a supervised homeroom period, with research assistants present to answer procedural questions but not to interpret item content. Students completed the SMES and PSQI on paper; APC scores were obtained from school records with student and parental consent and linked to survey responses using an anonymised participant code. Data were analysed using Pearson product-moment correlations to examine the bivariate relationships between social media use, sleep quality, and academic performance, followed by a multiple regression analysis with academic performance (APC) as the outcome variable and social media use (SMES) and sleep quality (PSQI) entered as simultaneous predictors, in order to estimate the unique contribution of each variable while controlling for the other.

Results & Discussion

Descriptive statistics showed a mean SMES score of 32.4 (SD = 7.8), a mean PSQI score of 9.6 (SD = 3.2, indicating poor sleep quality overall, as PSQI scores above 5 are conventionally classified as poor sleepers), and a mean APC score of 71.3 (SD = 11.5). Social media use was positively correlated with sleep quality scores (i.e., associated with worse sleep, since higher PSQI = worse sleep), r = .47, p < .001, indicating that students who reported heavier social media engagement also reported significantly more sleep disturbance. Sleep quality scores were negatively correlated with academic performance, r = −.54, p < .001, indicating that students with worse sleep quality (higher PSQI) tended to have lower academic performance scores. Social media use was also directly and negatively correlated with academic performance, r = −.39, p < .001.

The multiple regression model, with SMES and PSQI predicting APC simultaneously, was statistically significant, R² = .31, F(2, 309) = 69.42, p < .001, indicating that social media use and sleep quality together accounted for approximately 31% of the variance in academic performance scores. In this model, PSQI remained a significant unique predictor of APC (β = −.41, p < .001) even after controlling for SMES, while the unique contribution of SMES was smaller once PSQI was included (β = −.17, p = .004), consistent with the possibility that sleep disruption partially accounts for the association between social media use and academic performance. The authors note that, because the design was correlational and cross-sectional (all variables measured at a single time point), these findings describe an association consistent with the proposed sleep-mediated pathway but cannot establish that social media use causes poor sleep or that poor sleep causes lower academic performance; unmeasured variables and reverse-direction explanations remain possible.

Part A1 mark

Identify the research design used in this study and explain one limitation this design creates for establishing causation.

0 characters
Model Answer: This is a correlational (survey) design — the researchers measured social media use, sleep quality, and academic performance as they naturally occurred, without manipulating any variable or randomly assigning students to conditions. The key limitation is that a correlational design cannot establish causation: because no variable was manipulated, the direction of the relationship cannot be determined (poor sleep could lower academic performance, but low academic performance could also lead students to stay up later worrying or working, and heavy social media use could be a symptom rather than a cause of a broader pattern) and a third, unmeasured variable (a confound) could be producing the observed associations between all three variables.
Part B1 mark

Identify the independent variable(s) and the dependent variable examined in the regression analysis.

0 characters
Model Answer: In the multiple regression model, the predictor (independent) variables are social media use (measured by the SMES) and sleep quality (measured by the PSQI). The dependent (outcome) variable is academic performance, measured by the Academic Performance Composite (APC). Note that because the study is correlational rather than experimental, these are more precisely called "predictor" and "outcome" variables rather than a true manipulated independent variable and measured dependent variable.
Part C2 marks

Interpret r = −.54, p < .001 and R² = .31 from the Results section. Explain what each value means in the context of this study.

0 characters
Model Answer: r = −.54 describes the strength and direction of the linear relationship between sleep quality (PSQI) and academic performance (APC). The negative sign shows an inverse relationship: as PSQI scores increase (sleep gets worse), APC scores tend to decrease (academic performance gets lower). A magnitude of .54 indicates a moderately strong relationship (on a scale from 0 to 1). The p < .001 indicates that this correlation is statistically significant — the probability of observing a relationship this strong in the sample purely by chance, if there were truly no relationship in the population, is less than 0.1%, so the researchers can be confident the relationship is real rather than a random sampling fluctuation. R² = .31 comes from the regression model and means that social media use and sleep quality together statistically account for approximately 31% of the variance (individual differences) in students' academic performance scores; the remaining 69% of the variance is explained by other factors not measured in this study (e.g., prior achievement, motivation, family and school resources, or measurement error).
Part D1 mark

Identify one potential confounding variable not controlled for in this study and explain how it could affect the results.

0 characters
Model Answer: One plausible confounding variable is household stress or socioeconomic circumstances (for example, a chaotic home environment, food or housing insecurity, or a part-time job). Such conditions could independently increase both social media use (as an escape or coping behaviour), disrupt sleep (through noise, irregular schedules, or anxiety), and lower academic performance (through reduced study time, stress, or fewer academic resources at home) — meaning the observed correlations between social media use, sleep, and academic performance could be partly or wholly explained by this unmeasured third variable rather than any direct relationship between the two measured variables themselves.
Part E1 mark

A student concludes that reducing social media use will improve grades. Evaluate this conclusion using evidence from the study.

0 characters
Model Answer: This conclusion is not fully supported by the evidence presented. The study found a significant negative correlation between social media use and academic performance (r = −.39) and, more importantly, found that once sleep quality was included in the regression model, the unique contribution of social media use to academic performance dropped substantially (β = −.17) while sleep quality remained the stronger unique predictor (β = −.41). This pattern suggests the relationship between social media use and grades may operate largely through its effect on sleep rather than through a direct effect on studying or attention. Because the design is correlational, no causal claim can be made in either direction: it is equally possible that reducing social media use would improve sleep and indirectly improve grades, that a third variable (such as stress or motivation) is driving all three variables, or that students already struggling academically turn to social media more as an avoidant coping behaviour. An experiment that manipulates social media use directly would be needed before recommending it as an intervention to "improve grades."
Part F1 mark

Identify one ethical procedure described in the Method section and explain why it was necessary given the participants involved.

0 characters
Model Answer: The study obtained both written parental/guardian consent and written student assent before any data were collected. This two-step procedure was necessary because the participants were minors: minors are not legally able to provide fully informed consent on their own, so a parent or guardian must consent on the child's behalf, but the student's own agreement (assent) is also required so that the young person is not simply compelled to participate against their own wishes. The study also secured prior institutional review board (IRB) approval (Protocol #EDU-2025-118), which is necessary to ensure an independent body confirmed that the risks to these adolescent participants were minimal and that appropriate safeguards were in place before the research began.
Part G1 mark

Explain how this study could be redesigned as a true experiment to test a causal claim, and identify one practical or ethical challenge that redesign would face.

0 characters
Model Answer: To redesign this as a true experiment, researchers would need to randomly assign students to conditions rather than simply measuring existing behaviour — for example, randomly assigning one group of students to reduce social media use to a fixed limit (e.g., 30 minutes per day) for several weeks and a control group to continue their usual habits, then measuring sleep quality (PSQI) and academic performance (APC) afterwards in both groups. Random assignment would help ensure that pre-existing differences between groups (such as stress levels or motivation) are evenly distributed, allowing any resulting difference in sleep or performance to be more confidently attributed to the social media manipulation. A major practical and ethical challenge is that researchers cannot fully control or ethically restrict adolescents' private smartphone and social media use outside a lab setting — enforcing and verifying compliance with an assigned social media limit over several weeks would be difficult, and mandating that some students reduce a normal daily activity while others do not raises questions about fairness and the right to withdraw, since students might resent or resist an assigned restriction on their personal technology use.

Section C · Evidence-Based Question

Read the claim and the three sources below, then respond to Parts A, B(i), B(ii), C(i), C(ii). 6 marks total.

Claim

"The conclusions drawn from psychological research are shaped by the methods used, the populations studied, and the ethical constraints that govern what researchers are permitted to do."

Source 1 — A Brief Research-Literacy Training Programme and Detection of Methodological Flaws in News Articles

Introduction

Adolescents encounter psychological and health claims daily through news media and social platforms, yet formal instruction in evaluating research methodology is rarely part of secondary curricula. Prior work suggests that brief, targeted instruction in research literacy — teaching students to recognise small samples, missing control groups, and correlation-causation errors — can improve critical evaluation skills. This study tested whether a single-session training programme could measurably improve adolescents' ability to identify methodological flaws in real news articles reporting psychological findings, using a controlled experimental design so that any improvement could be attributed to the training itself rather than general maturation or repeated testing.

Participants

Sixty-eight Year 11 students (n = 68; 35 female, 33 male) from a single co-educational secondary school volunteered to participate, with parental consent and student assent obtained for all participants prior to enrolment. Mean age was 15.9 years (SD = 0.6). Students were randomly assigned using a computer-generated randomisation sequence to either the training group (n = 34) or an active control group (n = 34) that received an unrelated study-skills session of equal length. The study received institutional review board approval (Protocol #SCI-2024-092) prior to recruitment.

Method

All participants completed a 20-item Research Flaw Identification Test (RFIT; range 0–20, higher scores indicating better identification of methodological flaws such as small sample size, absence of a control group, and correlation misinterpreted as causation) as a pre-test. The training group then received a 45-minute structured lesson covering sampling, control groups, confounding variables, and correlation versus causation, illustrated with annotated news examples; the control group received a 45-minute study-skills session covering note-taking strategies, with no research-methods content. Both groups completed a parallel form of the RFIT (post-test) immediately afterward. Data were analysed using an independent-samples t-test comparing post-test gain scores (post-test minus pre-test) between groups.

Results & Discussion

Pre-test scores did not differ significantly between groups, t(66) = 0.41, p = .683, confirming baseline equivalence prior to the manipulation. Following the intervention, the training group showed a significantly larger gain in RFIT scores (M = 5.8, SD = 2.1) than the control group (M = 1.2, SD = 1.9), t(66) = 9.34, p < .001, Cohen's d = 2.27, indicating a very large effect of the brief training programme on adolescents' ability to detect methodological flaws in news articles reporting psychological research. Because participants were randomly assigned to conditions and groups were equivalent at pre-test, the researchers concluded that the training itself, rather than pre-existing differences between students, caused the improvement in flaw-detection performance.

Source 2 — Ethical Scrutiny and Long-Term Replication Success Across Published Studies

Introduction

Replication — the ability of independent researchers to reproduce an original finding — is a cornerstone of confidence in psychological science, yet many published findings fail to replicate. One under-examined possibility is that the intensity of ethical review a study undergoes is associated with broader aspects of methodological rigor (such as clearer protocols, better-justified sample sizes, and more carefully controlled procedures), which could in turn predict whether a finding successfully replicates. This longitudinal study examined whether researchers' self-reported level of ethical scrutiny during the IRB review process was associated with the likelihood that their original findings were later confirmed in independent replication attempts.

Participants

The dataset comprised 84 published psychology studies (n = 84) drawn from four peer-reviewed journals, each of which had been the subject of at least one independent, pre-registered replication attempt published within five years of the original study. For each original study, the corresponding author completed a retrospective Ethical Scrutiny Questionnaire (ESQ; range 8–40, higher scores indicating a more intensive IRB review process, including number of revision rounds requested, additional safeguards imposed, and review duration). Corresponding authors were contacted by email and provided informed consent to have their responses and study data included in this research; institutional review board approval for the tracking study was obtained (Protocol #REP-2020-054).

Method

Studies were tracked for five years following original publication to determine whether a successful independent replication (defined as a replication study reporting an effect in the same direction and reaching statistical significance at p < .05) was published. Each original study was coded as "replicated" or "not replicated" based on this criterion, and a Replication Success Score (0 = not replicated, 1 = replicated) was assigned. ESQ scores were correlated with replication outcomes using a point-biserial correlation, and a secondary analysis compared mean ESQ scores between replicated and non-replicated studies.

Results & Discussion

Mean ESQ score across all 84 studies was 24.6 (SD = 6.3). Studies that were later successfully replicated had significantly higher ESQ scores (M = 27.9, SD = 5.4) than studies that failed to replicate (M = 20.1, SD = 5.8). ESQ score was significantly correlated with replication success, r = .41, p < .001, indicating that studies which underwent more intensive ethical scrutiny during review were more likely to replicate successfully five years later. The authors suggest that the association may reflect a broader "rigor bundle": studies subjected to closer IRB scrutiny may also have undergone more careful methodological planning generally, though the study cannot establish that ethical scrutiny itself directly causes better replicability, since scrutiny level and methodological rigor were not independently manipulated.

Source 3 — Conformity Findings Compared Across WEIRD and Non-WEIRD Samples

Introduction

Much of the foundational research in psychology, including classic conformity studies, was conducted using participants from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies, raising questions about whether such findings generalise to populations with different cultural values around individualism and collectivism. This quasi-experimental study compared conformity rates on a standard line-judgment task between a WEIRD sample and a non-WEIRD sample to examine whether population characteristics shape the conclusions drawn about a well-established psychological phenomenon.

Participants

Participants were drawn from two existing, intact university cohorts rather than being individually randomly assigned to a sample: one WEIRD sample of 96 undergraduate students (mean age = 20.4, SD = 1.5) from a large research university in the United States, and one non-WEIRD sample of 91 undergraduate students (mean age = 20.9, SD = 1.7) from a university in a collectivist East Asian society, matched as closely as possible on age range and student status. Because participants belonged to pre-existing university cohorts in their respective countries, individual random assignment to sample was not possible; the comparison is therefore between intact groups rather than randomly equivalent groups. All participants provided informed consent, and the study received ethical approval from the review boards of both participating institutions (Protocol #CRC-2023-071).

Method

All participants completed a computerised adaptation of a standard line-judgment conformity task, in which participants viewed a comparison line alongside three reference lines and indicated which reference line matched it in length after seeing confederate-simulated majority responses that were incorrect on critical trials. Conformity was quantified using a Conformity Rate Score (CRS; range 0–100, representing the percentage of critical trials on which the participant conformed to the incorrect majority judgment). Analysis compared mean CRS between the WEIRD and non-WEIRD samples using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with effect size reported as eta-squared (η²).

Results & Discussion

The non-WEIRD sample showed a significantly higher mean conformity rate (M = 58.3, SD = 14.2) than the WEIRD sample (M = 36.7, SD = 13.6), F(1, 185) = 116.85, p < .001, η² = .39, indicating a large effect of sample/cultural context on conformity behaviour. The authors note that because participants were drawn from pre-existing intact cohorts rather than individually randomly assigned, cultural background is confounded with numerous other uncontrolled differences between the two universities and student populations (such as institutional norms, task familiarity, or unmeasured demographic differences), so the study cannot isolate cultural individualism/collectivism as the sole cause of the conformity difference. The findings nonetheless demonstrate that a phenomenon considered a robust, generalisable finding within WEIRD samples may produce substantially different conclusions when tested in non-WEIRD populations, underscoring that the populations studied shape the conclusions researchers are able to draw.

Identify the research design used in Source 1 and explain one strength of this design for testing the claim.

Model Answer: Source 1 uses a true experimental design: participants were randomly assigned to a training group or an active control group, and the researchers manipulated whether students received the research-literacy training. A key strength of this design for testing the claim is that random assignment produces groups that are equivalent at baseline (confirmed here by the non-significant pre-test difference, t(66) = 0.41, p = .683), which allows the researchers to attribute the large post-training gain (d = 2.27) specifically to the training method used rather than to pre-existing differences between students — directly supporting the claim that the methods used shape the conclusions a study can draw, since only the experimental design allows a causal conclusion about the training's effect.

Model Answer: Source 2 supports the claim that ethical constraints shape research conclusions: studies that underwent more intensive ethical scrutiny during IRB review (higher ESQ scores) were significantly more likely to replicate successfully over the following five years, r = .41, p < .001, with replicated studies averaging notably higher ESQ scores (M = 27.9) than non-replicated studies (M = 20.1). Part B(ii)2 marks

Name one specific psychological concept and fully explain how it accounts for the evidence you cited in Part B(i).

0 characters
Model Answer: The concept of research rigor as a mediating factor (methodological quality that underlies both ethical review outcomes and replicability) explains the Source 2 evidence. Research rigor refers to the overall care, precision, and control exercised in designing and conducting a study — including clearly justified sample sizes, well-operationalised variables, and carefully controlled procedures. A study that undergoes more intensive IRB scrutiny is typically required to justify its procedures, sample, and safeguards in greater detail, which tends to surface and correct methodological weaknesses before data collection begins. This means that ethical scrutiny does not directly cause replication success on its own; instead, it functions as an indirect marker of a broader "bundle" of methodological rigor — a study built carefully enough to satisfy a demanding ethics board is also more likely to have used adequate sample sizes, appropriate controls, and precise operational definitions, all of which independently make a finding more likely to be genuine (rather than a false positive) and therefore more likely to replicate. This directly connects the ethical-constraints evidence (r = .41) to the claim: the ethical process a study passes through is intertwined with the methodological quality that ultimately shapes whether its conclusions hold up.
Part C(i)1 mark

Use evidence from Source 3 to identify a limitation or complication of the claim.

0 characters
Model Answer: Source 3 complicates the claim by showing that even when a psychological phenomenon (conformity) is studied with a fairly standard task, the conclusion drawn depends heavily on the population sampled — conformity rates were significantly higher in the non-WEIRD sample (M = 58.3) than the WEIRD sample (M = 36.7), F(1, 185) = 116.85, p < .001, η² = .39. Part C(ii)1 mark

Name one specific psychological concept and fully explain how it accounts for the complication identified in Part C(i).

0 characters
Model Answer: The concept of a confounding variable in quasi-experimental (intact-group) designs explains this complication. A confounding variable is a factor other than the one being studied that varies systematically alongside the variable of interest, making it impossible to determine which factor is responsible for an observed difference. Because Source 3 compared two pre-existing, intact university cohorts rather than randomly assigning individuals to a "WEIRD" or "non-WEIRD" condition, cultural/population background is entangled with numerous other differences between the two universities and student bodies — such as differing institutional norms around classroom participation, unmeasured demographic composition, or differences in familiarity with laboratory tasks. Any of these uncontrolled factors could plausibly account for part or all of the large conformity difference (η² = .39), not just individualism versus collectivism. This shows precisely why the claim must be qualified: population differences do shape conclusions, but without random assignment, a confounding variable can never be fully ruled out as the true explanation for a between-group difference, limiting how strongly researchers can interpret the population effect itself.
